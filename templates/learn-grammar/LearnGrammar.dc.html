Ser vs estar, finally clear

Two verbs, one meaning in English: "to be." One short rule tells them apart. Learn it, hear it, then drill it until it's automatic.

Mateo C. · Native teacher, Medellín · Updated Jun 9, 2026
{{ t.label }}
The short version
Ser

What something is: identity, origin, job, time.

Estar

How or where it is right now: mood, location, condition.

1Location is always estar, even for buildings.
2Feelings are always estar: estoy bien, never soy bien.
3Some adjectives change meaning with each verb.

The rule in two words

Description

Occupation

Time & date

Origin

Relationship

ESTAR → PLACE
Position
Estoy sentado. I'm seated.
Location
Está en la cocina. She's in the kitchen.
Action (-ing)
Estoy comiendo. I'm eating.
Condition
Estás enfermo. You're sick.
Emotion
Estoy feliz. I'm happy.
Common mistake

Location is always estar

Locations feel permanent, so learners reach for ser. But a place is a state, even for a building.

✓El estadio está en el centro.
✕El estadio es en el centro.

Exception: events use ser. La fiesta es en mi casa.

Questions learners ask

Is it "estoy bien" or "soy bien"?+

Estoy bien. Feelings and current states always take estar.

Why is location estar if a building never moves?+

Spanish treats physical location as a state, not an identity. Events are the exception: la fiesta es en mi casa.

Ser or estar for "dead"?+

Estar: está muerto. Counterintuitive, but grammatically it's a state.

Ser or estar for "married"?+

Both are heard. Está casado is slightly more common in Latin America.
